The Satisficing Voter Model

How do epistemic noise and participation friction affect voting methods?

What is this?

Standard VSE simulations assume perfect voters: everyone knows their preferences and fills out their ballot completely. Real voters face two kinds of imperfection:

📚 Epistemic Noise  t

A voter may not know their own utilities — especially for unfamiliar candidates. The knowledge parameter $t \in [0,1]$ interpolates between pure noise ($t=0$) and perfect self-knowledge ($t=1$).

$$\tilde{u}_i = t \cdot u_i + (1-t) \cdot \eta_i, \quad \eta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{u}, \sigma_u^2)$$

⚡ Participation Friction  ℓ

Even a well-informed voter may not fill out their entire ballot, due to ballot fatigue. The energy parameter $\ell \in [0,1]$ controls what fraction of candidates each voter considers: $K = \max(1, \lceil \ell \cdot m \rceil)$.

$$K = \max\!\left(1,\ \left\lceil \ell \cdot m \right\rceil\right)$$

VSE (Voter Satisfaction Efficiency) measures how often a method elects the utility-maximizing candidate, normalised so 0 = random winner and 1 = always optimal.

How it works

Spatial model & utility

Voters and candidates are placed uniformly at random in $[-1,1]^d$, where $d$ is the selected spatial dimension. True utility is $u_i(c) = -\|v_i - p_c\|^2$ — negative squared distance. The utility-maximising candidate is $c^* = \arg\max_c \frac{1}{n}\sum_i u_i(c)$.

Noise model

Each voter's perceived utility is $\tilde{u}_i = t \cdot u_i + (1-t) \cdot \eta_i$ where $\eta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{u}, \sigma_u^2)^m$ is drawn once per voter and held fixed. This means even noisy voters have internally consistent beliefs.

Energy & considered set

Each voter considers only their top $K = \max(1, \lceil \ell \cdot m \rceil)$ candidates by perceived utility. Opinions on the remaining $m - K$ candidates are not expressed.

Ballot construction

Plurality: vote for the single top perceived candidate.
RCV / Borda / Condorcet: rank the considered set in order of $\tilde{u}_i$.
Score / STAR: normalise $\tilde{u}_i$ within the considered set to $[0,5]$.
Approval: approve candidates in the considered set whose $\tilde{u}_i$ exceeds the voter's global personal mean (computed over all $m$ candidates).

How winners are counted (including Condorcet)

Plurality: most top-choice votes wins.
Approval: most approvals wins.
Borda: ranked candidates get $K, K-1, \ldots, 1$ points (unranked get 0); highest total wins.
Score: highest total 0-5 score wins.
STAR: top two by total score advance; winner is preferred by more voters in the pairwise runoff based on their scores.
RCV: eliminate the lowest first-choice candidate each round; transfer to next ranked active choice; exhausted ballots drop out.
Condorcet: counted via Minimax: build pairwise margins from rankings and choose the candidate with the smallest worst pairwise defeat. This coincides with the true Condorcet winner when one exists, but still produces a result when there are cycles.

VSE formula

$$\text{VSE}_M(t,\ell) = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{SW}(w_M)] - \mathbb{E}[\text{SW}(\text{random})]}{\mathbb{E}[\text{SW}(c^*)] - \mathbb{E}[\text{SW}(\text{random})]}$$ Measured using true utilities even though voters act on $\tilde{u}$.

The Saturation Property (Approval)

Approval has a structural advantage: once $K \geq k_i^*$ (voter $i$'s natural approval count), the ballot is identical to the full-energy ballot. For a typical voter $\mathbb{E}[k_i^*] \approx m/2$, so approval "saturates" near $\ell = 0.5$ on average. Ranking/scoring methods lose information monotonically as $\ell$ decreases.

Read Conclusions

Ranked Choice Voting (RCV) performs surprisingly poorly across the board. Its VSE improvement over Plurality is quite small, particularly proportional to the administrative cost it has over methods like Approval, STAR, and Condorcet. The latter three methods all significantly outperform RCV across the board, and would have a lower cost to implement.

Borda performs exceptionally well under honest conditions, Borda is known for being particularly vulnerable to strategic voting, and is thus not generally seriously considered for political elections.

Similar to Yee diagrams, Approval tends to perform extremely similarly to Condorcet methods. There is a generally consistent gap where Condorcet is slightly better, apart from highly informed but low-energy voters where Approval is slightly better. However, the gap is generally quite small, and Approval is considerably simpler to implement and explain than Condorcet methods.

Overall, Approval is generally the most robust method to voter imperfections, maintaining high relative performance even under extreme noise and low energy. In particular, Approval becomes the best method when voters are informed but are easily exhausted by the ballot, and remains competitive even when voters are both uninformed and easily exhausted.

The fact that Approval does so well under low energy, whereas STAR and SCORE have major performance drops, has multiple implications:

  • It suggests that bullet voting is even less of a problem for Approval than many critics assume, since even if voters refuse to approve more than a few candidates, Approval can still maintain excellent performance. This is likely because the utility maximizer is probably among most voters' top two or three choices, so as long as voters can approve at least their top few candidates, the best candidate still generally earns enough approvals to win. Ranked methods like Borda and Condorcet also perform about as well as Approval for likely similar reasons, but RCV still generally underperforms.
  • Second, it means that more complex scoring methods like STAR and SCORE require voters to express preferences for more candidates in order to outperform Approval. If voters are likely to only fill out part of their ballot, or truncate their scores to just a few candidates, then the theoretical advantages of STAR/SCORE seen in VSE simulations with full ballots may not materialise in practice.

It should be mentioned that Approval naturally has a structural advantage in this model due to the way the considered set and approval threshold interact, which may contribute to its strong performance under low energy. However, this structural advantage is likely to be more representative of real-world conditions, where voters often are not perfectly informed and may not fill out their entire ballot. Thus, this structural advantage may actually be a point in Approval's favor in terms of practical considerations for how real voters behave, rather than an unfair quirk of the model.

This model also does not include strategic voting, which is likely to be more of a problem for a method like Borda. However, while Approval is claimed to be vulnerable to strategic voting (ex. heavy bullet voting), based on Approval's strong performance even under low energy, it seems plausible that Approval's performance would degrade much less under strategic voting than methods like Borda; especially given Approval's competitive VSE even under heavy strategy. However, this is an open question that would require further modeling to explore.

Further, we can see that overall, Approval remains competitive with more complex methods with or without noise, and even outperforms them in some scenarios. This suggests that the potential gains in outcomes that more complex methods like STAR or Condorcet can provide in ideal conditions, as shown in VSE simulations, may not bear out in real-world conditions where voters are imperfect. Instead, what is likely to be gained from moving from Approval to a more complex method is more administrative complexity and cognitive load for voters. Further, the results are harder to interpret and defend from a democratic legitimacy perspective, compared to Approval where there is no secondary data to spin.

Methods like STAR might be more suited to high-information, high-engagement elections, such as a decision by a committee of experts or a city council, where the participants have strong incentives to fill out their ballots carefully and fully. Whereas, STAR might be less suited to electing a city council where voters are less familiar with the candidates and less motivated to completely and thoughtfully fill out their ballots. In the latter case, the simplicity and robustness of Approval might make it a better choice, even if STAR has higher theoretical performance under perfect conditions.

Approval voting appears to be a low-friction, robust voting method that achieves the same general quality of outcomes as more complex methods across a wide range of voter imperfection scenarios, while being low-cost to implement, easy to count, easy to explain, easy to interpret, and easy to defend from a democratic legitimacy perspective.
